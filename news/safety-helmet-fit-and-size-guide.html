Safety Helmet Fit and Size Guide: Measure Your Head and Adjust the Suspension

2026-11-18

Safety helmet size guide: measure your head in centimetres, find your size, and adjust the suspension so the helmet stays put all day.

A safety helmet that fits stays on, stays comfortable and stays protective; a helmet that does not fit gets taken off, pushed back or worn loose — and a helmet that is not worn protects nobody. Measure your head with a soft tape, find your size in the table below, then adjust the suspension until the helmet sits level and snug. The whole job takes two minutes, and it is the most useful two minutes in helmet buying.

1. How to measure your head

Take a soft, flexible tape measure — the cloth kind, not a metal one, because a metal tape does not follow the shape of the head. Wrap it around the widest part of the head, about 2.5 cm above the eyebrows and just above the ears, keeping the tape level all the way around. Pull it snug without squeezing the skin or compressing the hair, and read the number in centimetres to the nearest millimetre.

Measure twice. If the two readings differ, take the average, or measure a third time — a half-centimetre error is the difference between two sizes, and most size steps are only 1–2 cm wide. Measure without a beanie, a cap or a hair pad that will not be worn on the job, because anything under the tape adds centimetres that are not really there. Measure in the middle of the day if you can: heads do not swell as much as hands do, but a measurement taken calmly beats one taken in a rush.

Write the number down and send it with your inquiry, not just a size letter. A size letter means different things on different charts, but a head circumference in centimetres travels across every size system without confusion. The supplier can then confirm which size matches that measurement for the helmet you order — and if your crew's heads span a wide range, the measurements tell the supplier how many of each size to send, instead of a guess.

Most adult heads fall between 53 cm and 64 cm, and the average adult head measures about 57 cm — a size M. The table is a general published sizing reference, and different makers' charts vary slightly, so treat it as the starting point and confirm the size with a try-on, not as a fixed rule. If your measurement falls between two sizes, try the smaller size first: a helmet that is slightly snug can be adjusted, while a helmet that is slightly loose will slide.

The hat-size column is there for buyers who order by hat size. Divide the head circumference in inches by 3.14 and you get the hat size: a 22-inch head is a size 7. Most helmet suspensions adjust across the whole range in the table, from hat size 6½ to 8 — that is, from 52 cm to 64 cm — so one suspension design covers the whole crew. A few makers sell a small or an extra-large shell for heads outside that range; if anyone on your crew measures below 52 cm or above 64 cm, say so in the inquiry.

3. Adjusting the suspension: the fit is in the inside, not the shell

The shell is the hard outside; the suspension is the cradle inside that actually holds the helmet on the head. A helmet's fit is decided by the suspension, not by the shell, so a helmet that feels wrong is usually a suspension that has not been adjusted, not a shell that is the wrong size.

Start with the headband. Loosen it, put the helmet on, and tighten the band until the helmet sits level — not tipped forward over the eyes, not tipped back off the forehead — and stays put when you bend forward and nod. The band should feel snug all the way around, without pressing hard in one place. A band that presses in one place means the suspension is sitting unevenly, not that the helmet is too small; loosen it, centre the helmet, and tighten it again evenly.

Then set the height. Most suspensions let the cradle sit higher or lower inside the shell. The helmet should sit low enough to cover the forehead and the back of the head, with a small gap between the shell and the top of the head — that gap is part of how the helmet works, so do not push the shell down onto the head. If the helmet wobbles side to side, the band is too loose; if it leaves a red line after a few minutes, it is too tight.

Check the fit the way the job will check it: bend forward, look up, turn your head side to side, and walk a few steps. The helmet should stay where you put it. If it slides forward over your eyes when you bend, tighten the band one notch and try again. Do this check for every worker, not just one — heads differ in shape as well as size, and a size that fits one worker's round head can press on another worker's longer head. Shell shape is the second thing to check after the measurement: a correct circumference on the wrong shape still feels wrong, which is why every good fitting ends with a try-on, not with a chart.

4. Chin strap, accessories and the jobs that change the fit

A chin strap keeps the helmet on when the worker bends, climbs or looks up — and on windy sites, it is what stops the helmet leaving the head altogether. If the job involves working at height, climbing, or bending over open edges, order the chin strap with the helmet and fit it so it sits snug under the chin without pulling the helmet down. If the job is flat ground in still air, the strap is optional — but it costs little, and a helmet that falls off a scaffold is a helmet that is not there when it is needed.

Accessories change the fit too. Ear muffs that clip to the helmet, a face shield, or a headlamp bracket all add weight and change how the helmet sits. Fit the helmet with the accessories on, not without them — a helmet that fits bare and slides with muffs attached is a helmet that was fitted wrong. Goggles fit the same way: try the helmet and the goggles together, because the goggle strap and the helmet suspension share the same space on the head, and the two have to sit together without pushing each other out of place. Safety goggles are available as a kit add-on with the helmet and vest; the kit is fitted as one set, not as three separate pieces.

Vented and non-vented shells take the same sizes, and the measurement is the same for both — the vents are holes in the shell, not a different shape. Choose vented where the crew works in heat and needs airflow, and non-vented where the job involves splashes or falling liquid from above. The choice is about the job, not the head, so it does not change the size.

5. How to order: send the measurements, trial the fit, then order in bulk

Start by measuring the crew. Write down each worker's head circumference in centimetres, and count how many fall into each size. Send the supplier that list — the sizes and the counts — together with the helmet style, the shell type (vented or not), and whether chin straps are needed. A complete inquiry reads like this: "10 helmets, sizes M × 6 and L × 4, vented, with chin straps". That one line carries everything the supplier needs to quote, and it leaves no room for a wrong size run.

Trial the fit before the bulk order. Order one helmet in each size the crew needs, and have the workers who will wear them try them on the real job — bending, climbing, looking up — for a full shift. Check three things: whether the helmet stays put, whether the band presses anywhere after a few hours, and whether the helmet sits well with the goggles and the vest. A trial that is not worn on the real job tells you nothing — a desk fitting is not a shift test.

One last check before you confirm: ask how to look after the helmets and when to replace them. A helmet that has taken a knock, been left in the sun, or been stored badly may look fine and still need replacing, and the replacement point is not visible from outside. Our helmet care and replacement guide covers what to check and when to swap a helmet out. The right size, fitted properly and replaced on time, is what turns a size chart into a helmet that protects.

FAQ

How do I measure my head for a safety helmet? Wrap a soft tape around the widest part of the head, about 2.5 cm above the eyebrows and just above the ears, keeping the tape level. Read the number in centimetres, measure twice, and send the measurement — not just a size letter — with your inquiry.

What if my measurement falls between two sizes? Try the smaller size first. A helmet that is slightly snug can be adjusted through the suspension, while a helmet that is slightly loose will slide. Confirm the choice with a try-on on the real job.

Do vented and non-vented helmets take different sizes? No. The vents are holes in the shell, not a different shape, so the measurement and the size table are the same for both. Choose vented for heat and airflow, non-vented for splashes and falling liquid.

How tight should the suspension be? Snug all the way around, without pressing hard in one place. The helmet should stay put when you bend forward and nod, and sit level — not tipped forward over the eyes or back off the forehead. If it leaves a red line after a few minutes, it is too tight.

Do I need a chin strap? Order one if the job involves working at height, climbing, bending over open edges, or wind. On flat ground in still air it is optional, but it costs little and stops the helmet leaving the head when it is needed most.

What head sizes do helmet suspensions cover? Most suspensions adjust from hat size 6½ to 8, which is 52 cm to 64 cm around the head. If anyone on your crew measures below 52 cm or above 64 cm, say so in the inquiry — some makers sell a small or an extra-large shell for heads outside that range.
